This chapter presents the methodological framework used to design, develop, and evaluate
QualCheck. All methodological choices were made in response to the gaps found in literature reviewed, starting with using BERT-base-uncased As the encoding backbone to the

Research Design
This study employed the Constructive Research Design, a research approach commonly
used in computer science and information technology studies that focus on designing and
developing a practical solution to an existing problem. Constructive research emphasizes
the creation of a functional artifact or system intended to address identified limitations in
current practices. In this study, the construct developed is QualCheck: A Rubric-Guided
Semantic Evaluation System for Academic Responses and Code Reports Using Natural
Language Processing (NLP). The system was designed to address the limitations of existing automated grading tools that primarily rely on keyword matching and lack rubricguided semantic evaluation across different academic output formats. Through the integration of transformer-based language models, semantic similarity scoring, and rubric-based
relevance classification, QualCheck aims to provide a more consistent, interpretable, and
semantically aware assessment process for academic responses.
The study focused on the design, development, and evaluation of the QualCheck system
32
using a structured and systematic process. A labeled academic response dataset composed
of short answers, essays, and code reports served as the basis dataset for threshold calibration and evaluation of the semantic classification process. Short answers consisted of
approximately 1–3 sentences or fewer than 100 words, essays contained multiple paragraphs or exceeded 100 words requiring analytical discussion, while code reports referred
to technical written explanations accompanying programming outputs. The system development process included rubric construction, dataset organization, BERT-based encoding,
cosine similarity computation, and relevance classification into Fully Relevant, Partially
Relevant, and Irrelevant categories. The effectiveness of the system was evaluated using
classification accuracy, semantic alignment scoring, and reliability measures to determine
whether the proposed construct provided a clear, organized, and pedagogically meaningful
automated assessment process.
Theorems, Algorithms, and Mathematical Framework
In this section, the algorithms and mathematical models of the computational core of the
QualCheck system are discussed in detail. This system is based on three components that
are all interrelated, and they include transformer-based language encoding with the usage
of BERT, calculation of semantic scoring by the use of cosine similarity, and a rubric-based
classification algorithm to classify relevance.
Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)
BERT is a language model based on transformers trained on large text collections that
learn deep contextual representations of words by attending to both right and left context
of each word in a sequence at the same time [3]. In contrast to previous unidirectional
models, BERT has a self-attention mechanism, where each token in the input focuses on
all the other tokens in the input, generating embeddings that encode rich semantic and
33
contextual information. It has been established that BERT-based models are always better
than baseline neural networks in automated academic scoring tasks, and their benefits are
language and domain-independent [9].

Cosine Similarity
Following the encoding of student response into fixed-size vector representations by
BERT, the extent of semantic alignment between the two is quantified with Cosine Similarity. Cosine similarity is a distance measure that quantifies the similarity of two vectors in a
high-dimensional space by the cosine of the angle between the two vectors, not the distance
between the two vectors, which is especially useful in semantic embedding comparisons
where the meaning of the direction of a vector is encoded in its direction.[8]

derived from the BERT [CLS] output, the cosine similarity score s is defined as:

a negative score implies opposing semantic directions. Regarding academic evaluation,
scores would be within the range [0, 1] as academic responses and their questions are linked
to each other with positive semantics [2].

dataset entry consists of four structured fields (1) an academic prompt or task instruction,
(2) a student response to the prompt, (3) a rubric descriptor specifying the criteria for full
relevance, and (4) an assigned relevance label (Fully Relevant, Partially Relevant, or Irrelevant) assigned by qualified raters following a standardized labeling protocol.The finalized
dataset was partitioned into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets using
stratified random sampling to ensure proportional representation of all three relevance cate-

gories across all splits. The training subset was used exclusively for fine-tuning the BERTbase-uncased model. The validation subset was utilized for the calibration of classification
thresholds θ1 and θ2, as well as for monitoring fine-tuning performance to prevent overfitting. The test subset served as the held-out set for the final evaluation of the QualCheck
system’s performance and generalizability.
Phase 2: BERT Encoding and Embedding Generation. Each academic prompt, rubric
descriptor, and student response was encoded using the pretrained BERT-base-uncased
transformer model. The combined prompt-rubric input was formatted using the standard
BERT multi-segment structure as: [CLS] prompt [SEP] rubric [SEP], and the student
response was encoded separately as: [CLS] response [SEP]. Both inputs were processed
through the 12-layer BERT-base-uncased architecture to produce 768-dimensional contextual embeddings extracted from the final hidden state of the [CLS] token. These sentencelevel embeddings served as the vector representations P (for the combined prompt-rubric
input) and R (for the student response), which were subsequently used in the cosine similarity computation defined in Equation 3.2.The model was fine-tuned on the training subset
using a standard supervised fine-tuning procedure with the AdamW optimizer, a learning
rate of 2e-5, and a maximum of 5 training epochs with early stopping based on validation
loss. Fine-tuning was conducted to adapt the model’s contextual representations to the specific linguistic patterns and rubric-defined relevance criteria present in academic responses
across all three supported output types.
Phase 3: Cosine Similarity Scoring. For each prompt-response pair in the dataset, semantic alignment was quantified by computing the cosine similarity between the BERT
[CLS] embeddings P and R using the formula defined in Equation 3.2. The resulting
similarity score s ∈ [0, 1] represents the continuous semantic alignment score for the pair,
where values approaching 1 indicate high conceptual correspondence and values approaching 0 indicate minimal semantic overlap. All computed similarity scores were stored systematically for use in the threshold calibration and relevance classification procedures of

Phase 4.
Phase 4: Threshold Calibration and Relevance Classification. The two relevance classification thresholds, θ1 and θ2, were determined through an exhaustive grid search conducted
on the validation set. Candidate threshold pairs were evaluated at a step size of 0.01 across
the full range of observed cosine similarity scores. The pair that maximized the weighted
average of the Weighted F1-Score and overall classification accuracy on the validation set
was selected as the operational thresholds. These calibrated thresholds were then applied to
the held-out test set using the rubric-based classification function C(s) defined in Equation
3.3, assigning each student response to one of three relevance categories: Fully Relevant
(s ≥ θ1), Partially Relevant (θ2 ≤ s < θ1), or Irrelevant (s < θ2). These categories align
with rubric-defined performance levels established by the instructors, ensuring that the automated classification output remains criterion-referenced and pedagogically interpretable.
Phase 5: System Evaluation. QualCheck was evaluated on the held-out test set across
three primary dimensions: classification performance, semantic alignment scoring quality,
and cross-output-type consistency. Classification performance was assessed using a multiclass confusion matrix from which Overall Accuracy, Precision, Recall, macro-average
F1-Score, and Weighted F1-Score were derived per output type and in aggregate, with a
minimum Weighted F1-Score of 0.80 set as the viability threshold. Semantic alignment
scoring quality was measured using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square
Error (RMSE), computed between QualCheck’s continuous cosine similarity scores and
normalized human rubric ratings. Score-level agreement between automated and human
assessments was further quantified using the Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r), with a
target threshold of r ≥ 0.70. Cross-output-type consistency was examined through a oneway ANOVA, supplemented by Tukey HSD post-hoc tests where statistically significant
differences were identified (p ≤ 0.05). Descriptive statistics including mean, median, standard deviation, and interquartile range were computed per output type and per relevance
class, with score distributions visualized using histograms and box plots to assess class
43
separability. All results were further compared against two baseline systems: Baseline 1, a
TF-IDF cosine similarity approach without BERT or rubric integration, representing conventional keyword-matching methods; and Baseline 2, a BERT cosine similarity approach
without rubric-based threshold classification, representing single-component transformer
approaches. Improvements over both baselines were reported as delta values (∆Weighted
F1-Score and ∆MAE) to quantify the contribution of QualCheck’s rubric-guided unified
evaluation pipeline.

Theory of Automated Essay Scoring (AES).Automated Essay Scoring (AES) is defined
as the application of computing systems to receive and mark written academic assignments
using the analysis of the linguistic, semantic, and structural characteristics of the work,
which minimizes the human assessment through the time-consuming manual method of
grading [7]. The systematic review of deep learning-based approaches to AES revealed
that the area has evolved past the initial rule-based and statistical models to deeper semantic relations captured by transformer-based models, with specific increases in reliability
and consistency in scoring student writing [10]. A wider review of AES issues, methods,
and future perspectives established that the transition to end-to-end deep learning representations has become the overwhelming trend in the field, despite domain generalisation
and interpretability issues continuing to be actively researched [15]. This theory explains
the existence of the research problem because it demonstrates that while automated scoring systems have significantly advanced, the majority of existing systems are configured to
assess only one type of academic output, most often essays, and cannot support the entire
variety of academic outputs that students write in various courses and disciplines [10, 15,
50
5]. Computer science and other technical students are producing short answers, essays, and
code reports on a regular basis in the same course, yet no clearly shown unified automated
assessment infrastructure performs all three types of outputs on the same semantic evaluation pipeline. The AES theory provides the basis for developing QualCheck as a system
that extends automated scoring to cover short answers, essays, and code reports within a
single unified framework.
Theory of natural language processing, Natural Language Processing (NLP) refers to
a sub-domain of artificial intelligence which allows the programmed computer to process,
analyse, and comprehend the human language with the help of identifying patterns, associations, and meanings within text messages, which helps in classifying texts, finding semantically similar texts, and retrieving information [10]. With NLP techniques, automated
systems are able to go beyond basic word matching and are able to analyze the semantic
content of student responses. With this development, automated grading systems can find
out whether an answer conceptually matches the desired question or the goal of learning
[10].
Initial studies of automated essay scoring concentrated more on statistical and rulebased methods which considered measurable textual characteristics like word frequency,
sentence length, and lexical similarity. These methods were however not that effective in
determining whether one response really indicated conceptual knowledge. A lot of systems
could recognize structural aspects of writing and had a hard time determining whether a student had actually been in earnest when dealing with the concepts that a question or prompt
requested [15]. This prompted researchers to explore more sophisticated machine learning
methods that would be able to elicit more intense linguistic and semantic insights in the
response of the student [15]. Survey of automated student assessment and evaluation with
machine learning showed that most current systems use pre-structured performance information instead of assessing the semantic value of written academic work, and therefore it
should be emphasized that NLP-based tools are required to be able to analyze the seman-
51
tic value of written academic work to evaluate the conceptual quality and relevance [6].
This theory directly explains why the research problem of unreliable and inconsistent automated grading exists: systems that rely on keyword matching and superficial text features
cannot determine the true semantic correspondence between a student response and the
academic prompt, leading to misclassification of valid answers. QualCheck addresses this
by employing NLP-based semantic analysis to evaluate the conceptual meaning of student
responses rather than their surface textual features.
Theory of Transformer-Based Language Models for Text Evaluation, Transformerbased language models signify a significant technology leap in automated academic assessment. They adopt the self-attention mechanisms to model the contextual dependencies
over whole text sequences and generate intimately contextualized word representations that
model subtle semantic associations [9]. Among the most significant technological advances
in the field of NLP, the introduction of transformer-based language models should be mentioned. These are models that make use of mechanisms of attention to analyse contextual
links between words within a sentence or document. Transformer architectures also make
machines reason about the interaction of words with each other in a larger context, and
systems are able to find similarities in meaning even when other words are used, or the sentence structure is different [9]. Comparative analysis of pretrained transformer models to
automatically grade essays in the Indonesian language discovered that BERT-based model
network constructions were effective in all instances over baseline neural networks, a fact
confirming that the advantages of transformer pre-training are interchangeable across languages and educational sectors [9]. A study of automated scoring open-response scoring
in mathematics employed the Sentence-BERT model to generate semantic representations
of sentences, and discovered that it outperforms previously reported benchmarks in several
evaluation measures and that it is able to score responses containing a correct line of reasoning expressed using different vocabulary [2]. This theory provides the scientific basis
for QualCheck’s use of transformer-based language models as the encoding backbone for
52
computing semantic alignment between rubric descriptors, and student responses.
Theory of Rubric-Based Assessment, A rubric is a scoring guide or assessment instrument adopted in learning with the aim of assessing student work systematically and in a
uniform, transparent, and objective way [13]. Rubrics are guidelines or systematic instructions that express how the work of the students is to be rated in reference to certain learning
goals and performance thresholds. They assist in promoting fairness, transparency, and
uniformity in grading [7]. Studies of automated scoring of student answer scripts with
large language models have shown that evaluation scores are highly correlated with human
expert scores especially when structured evaluation standards, like rubric descriptions, are
given to the model to guide its scoring on particular dimensions of student performance
[11]. The researchers have determined that rubric-prompting significantly enhanced the
similarity and pedagogical significance of scores produced with the help of large language
models as opposed to free evaluation, which is in line with the design principle of incorporating structured criteria with automated evaluation systems [11]. Furthermore, studies
repeatedly indicate that automated systems with structured rubric standards give higher results of consistency, interpretability, and pedagogical value than systems with no rubric
standards [11].
This theory explains a critical dimension of the research problem: the majority of existing automated evaluation systems fail to use rubric-based criteria in their evaluation systems. Unless automated systems are coupled with a structured rubric, they may fail to discern the level of accomplishment of a response relative to the criteria defined by teachers
[7]. Combining semantic analysis with rubric-based evaluation might enhance the interpretability and reliability of automated grading systems in a substantial way. QualCheck
directly addresses this gap by integrating rubric descriptors into the semantic encoding and
classification stages of its evaluation pipeline, ensuring that automated scoring is criterionreferenced and aligned with instructor-defined performance standards.
Theory of Semantic Similarity Measurement, Semantic similarity is a quantitative mea-

sure of the degree of overlapping of meaning between two texts that includes not only the
vocabulary shared by texts but also the contextual and conceptual associations between the
words and phrases that the texts operate [1]. The measurement of semantic similarity is
used to determine the level of overlap of meaning between two text passages, including
both conceptual and contextual relationships that span beyond the shared words, and has
turned into a fundamental method of automated grading systems that are required to identify correct student responses using varying linguistic forms [8]. A study on semantic similarity between sentences with transformer-based deep learning has shown that BERT-like
models fine-tuned with metric learning methods, including a Siamese and a triplet network,
can be effective at determining whether two sentences are semantically equivalent despite
using totally different vocabulary [8]. It was shown that scoring in semantic representation
essentially lowers false negatives, namely instances of a student giving a correct answer to
which the system identifies the answer as incorrect due to vocabulary mismatch with the expected answer, which is a common issue in keyword-based pattern systems [2]. This theory
provides the direct mathematical and computational foundation for QualCheck’s semantic
alignment scoring, which uses cosine similarity between BERT-generated vector representations to quantify the degree of conceptual correspondence between student responses and
rubric-defined academic assessment.
